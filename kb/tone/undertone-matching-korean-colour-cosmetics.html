What the letters cover, and what they miss

N, W and C attach to a depth number: 21N, 23W, 21C. Warm leans yellow-golden, cool leans pink, neutral splits the difference. Missing from that scheme is olive — a green-grey cast that reads as neither warm nor cool and appears across South Asian, Southeast Asian, Middle Eastern, Mediterranean and African complexions. Also missing are the red-leaning undertones common in deeper skin. If a warm shade turns orange on you and a neutral turns grey, olive is usually the reason, and no Korean letter code will ever tell you that.

Personal colour and its limits

Personal colour is a large consumer phenomenon in Korea: a paid consultation sorts you into a season — spring warm, summer cool, autumn warm, winter cool — and the result guides clothing, hair and makeup choices. It is enjoyable and often flattering. It is also a styling framework developed around a fairly narrow range of complexions, applied by eye under studio lighting, with no diagnostic standing whatsoever. Treat a personal-colour result as a suggestion about which lipsticks you might enjoy, and never as a method for matching foundation.

The matching method that works

Swatch along the jaw and down onto the neck, never the inner arm or the back of the hand, both of which are usually lighter and differently toned than the face. Lay two adjacent shades side by side. Wait fifteen to twenty minutes, because bases settle and read deeper as volatile ingredients evaporate — judging a base wet is the commonest mistake there is, and it is what most people mistake for oxidation. Look in indirect daylight near a window. The right shade is the one that disappears, jawline included.

Fixing a near-miss

Small mismatches are worth rescuing rather than returning. Too light: mix in a deeper base or a pigment drop, then warm the perimeter with a cream bronzer. Too pink: a yellow or golden loose powder pulls it back. Too orange: a neutral or faintly cool powder, applied sparingly. Too grey is the one that rarely rescues, because it usually signals a formula heavy in white pigment or mineral filter rather than a wrong shade choice. Colour correctors help with localised tone under the base, not with a base that is fundamentally wrong.
