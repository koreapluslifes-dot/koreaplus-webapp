Korean haircare on textured hair: what the 두피 (dupi) obsession gives you, and what it does not

Take the scalp products, the oils and the deep-conditioning packs; leave the daily-shampoo assumption where you found it.

What Korean haircare was designed for

The Korean market treats the 두피 (dupi), the scalp, as skin and sells it skincare: exfoliating scalers, cooling tonics, ampoules, scalp masks, and shampoos formulated around sebum control. The hair itself is generally assumed to be fine to medium, straight or gently waved, frequently coloured or permed, and washed daily or close to it. Product textures follow that brief — light conditioners, watery essences, fast-absorbing oils, heat protectants for the straightener. It is a coherent system for the hair in front of it, and a poor fit for hair that is tightly curled and structurally dry.

What transfers well

Scalp care is the genuine export. Gentle scalp exfoliation, soothing tonics, and treating flaking or itch as a skin problem rather than a hair problem all apply regardless of curl pattern — and hair kept in protective styles often means a scalp that gets ignored for weeks. Camellia oil is a long-standing Korean hair ingredient and behaves like other light plant oils, useful for sealing and shine. Hair packs and ampoule treatments work fine as deep conditioners, left on far longer than the box suggests. So do heat protectants, which are indifferent to texture.

What does not

Daily washing is wrong for most coily and very dry hair, and shampoos designed for daily use can strip it badly. Scalp tonics loaded with menthol, camphor and alcohol feel bracing on an oily scalp and leave a dry one tight and irritated. Korean conditioners are usually too light to give the slip that detangling requires, so expect to keep a heavier conditioner for that job specifically. And several ranges lean hard on protein and keratin; hair that is already protein-sensitive turns stiff and snappy on that diet, so alternate with moisture-led treatments.

The claims to be sceptical about

Korea has a functional cosmetics category covering relief of hair-loss symptoms, and products carrying it are tested against that wording — a cosmetic claim about symptoms, not a treatment for hair loss. Scalp ampoules promising density or regrowth are running well ahead of what a leave-on cosmetic can support. Evidence that scalp exfoliation improves growth is thin; evidence that it makes a flaky scalp more comfortable is considerably better. If hair is thinning or shedding in a way that worries you, that is a doctor's question and an early one.
