Why burn-time sun advice does not transfer to deeper skin, and what does

⏱ 2 min read

Deeper skin still accumulates ultraviolet exposure, still pigments, and is affected by visible light in ways fair skin largely is not. The reason to wear sunscreen changes considerably; the conclusion that you should wear it does not.

The Fitzpatrick problem

The scale was devised to predict how skin responds to ultraviolet in a clinical setting, and its upper categories are defined largely by not burning, which collapses an enormous range of complexions into two crude boxes. Advice built on it inherits the flaw: burn times, shade schedules, warnings pitched at redness and peeling. If burning is not your experience, none of that describes a risk you recognise, and the reasonable conclusion is that the advice was written for somebody else. The advice is badly written. The underlying exposure is not somebody else's.

What you are actually protecting against

Three things, in rough order of how soon you will notice them. Pigmentation: ultraviolet re-darkens existing marks and melasma and drives new unevenness, which is the single most common complaint from people with deeper skin who use no sun protection. Photoageing: the loss of the look of firmness and evenness, accumulating quietly across decades. And visible light, particularly the blue-violet end, which appears to provoke pigment more readily in skin with more melanin — the reason tinted formulas containing iron oxides keep coming up in this context rather than in general sun advice.

Melanin is protection, not a sunscreen

Higher melanin does provide meaningful intrinsic protection against ultraviolet. That is not a myth, and it is why burning is rarer. What it is not is equivalent to wearing sunscreen: the protection it offers sits far below what a properly applied SPF product provides. Skin cancers do occur in people with deep skin, they often appear at sites people do not associate with sun exposure, and they are frequently found later than they should be. That is a reason to have anything new, changing or non-healing looked at by a doctor, not a reason for alarm.

What a workable routine looks like

A cast-free organic-filter sunscreen every morning, applied at the full recommended amount rather than a smear. A tinted or iron-oxide-containing option if you have melasma or mark easily, since that is where the visible-light argument actually bites. Reapplication whenever you are outdoors for long stretches, using whatever you will realistically reapply rather than the theoretically ideal product. And the unglamorous physical measures — a hat, sleeves, the shady side of the street — which cost nothing and outperform any bottle. Consistency beats sophistication here by a wide margin.
