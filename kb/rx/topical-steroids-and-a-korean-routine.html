Topical steroids: what the rest of the routine should and should not do

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Topical corticosteroids come in a range of potencies and are prescribed for defined situations and defined areas, with facial use handled especially carefully — none of which is something to work out from an article. Around them, the usual advice is a bland fragrance-free routine and a plain moisturiser, with actives paused while skin is flaring. How much, where and for how long is entirely the prescriber's territory, including any decision to stop.

Potency, site and why the label matters

Steroid creams are not interchangeable. Potency classes differ substantially, and clinicians choose differently for the face, the eyelids, body folds and thicker skin elsewhere, because absorption differs by site. A tube prescribed for a patch on someone's elbow is not automatically appropriate for a cheek. This is the most common informal-sharing error in skincare, and it is worth stating plainly: use what was prescribed to you, where it was prescribed for, and take leftover tubes back to a pharmacy rather than improvising with them later.

The unlabelled cream problem

Regulators in a number of countries have issued warnings about creams sold online and informally that were found to contain undeclared corticosteroids, often marketed for eczema, brightening or 'miracle' calming. The pattern is recognisable: dramatic early results, no ingredient list you can verify, no manufacturer you can trace, sold through social media. Korea's cosmetics rules require a full 전성분 (jeonseongbun, 'full ingredient list') on the packaging, so a product without one is already outside the system. If a cream works far too well and nobody can tell you why, be suspicious.

Steroid withdrawal, honestly

Topical steroid withdrawal is discussed intensely online and the discussion outruns the evidence. Some clinicians recognise a withdrawal pattern after prolonged potent use; others dispute how common it is and how it should be defined, and the published evidence is genuinely thin in both directions. What follows from that uncertainty is not self-tapering based on forum protocols, which is how flares get worse and skin infections get missed. If you are worried about how long you have been using something, that worry is a reason to book an appointment.

What Korean skincare does alongside

Moisturising, and staying out of the way. A non-foaming or low-foam cleanser, an unscented cream or ointment, and generous, frequent application are the standard supporting advice for inflamed skin. Korea's 저자극 (jeojageuk, 'low-irritation') and derma lines suit this well, provided you check for fragrance, which many otherwise gentle Korean products still carry. Sheet masks, acid toners, vitamin C and exfoliants generally wait. Sunscreen still matters, and a mineral-leaning formula is often more comfortable on skin that stings. Ask your prescriber how a moisturiser should be timed relative to the steroid.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How often should you exfoliate in a Korean routine?

Once or twice a week for most people, up to three times for oily and resilient skin. Frequency matters far more than strength — a mild acid used consistently beats a strong one that forces you to stop.
