Decide what each product is for

A useful exercise is to sort your shelf into four piles: cleanse, hydrate, moisturise, protect — and a fifth pile of actives. The first four are almost always compatible with a prescription, provided they are fragrance-light and gentle. The fifth is where conflicts live: acids, retinols, strong vitamin C, exfoliating pads, tingling ampoules. Most people discover the fifth pile is much bigger than they thought, because Korean products bury exfoliants inside things that look like toners and essences. Read ingredient lists, not category names.

One variable at a time

The reason clinicians dislike elaborate routines during treatment is not aesthetic snobbery. It is that when something flares, an eleven-product routine gives you eleven suspects and no way to narrow them. Introduce nothing new in the settling-in phase. After that, add one product, use it long enough to see how skin responds, and write down what it was and when. Dated notes sound obsessive until the day your dermatologist asks what changed and you can actually answer.

Where Korean routines genuinely add something

Comfort, adherence and sun protection — the three things that decide whether a prescription plan survives contact with real life. Korea's 진정 (jinjeong, 'calming') and barrier categories are deep and well made, the moisturiser range covers every weight from watery gel to ointment, and the sunscreen category is the best in the world at textures people will actually reapply. Those are not small contributions. They are just support rather than treatment, and the marketing that blurs the two is the thing to stay sceptical about.

The things that are never yours to change

Starting, stopping, pausing, buffering, alternating or 'saving' a prescription for a special occasion. Those are all clinical decisions, and adjusting them because a routine felt harsh last Tuesday is the most common way people lose months. If the plan is unbearable, that is a reason to go back and say so — prescribers can usually change the vehicle, the pairing or the approach. Nothing on this page substitutes for the instructions of the clinician who knows your skin.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.

How often should you exfoliate in a Korean routine?

Once or twice a week for most people, up to three times for oily and resilient skin. Frequency matters far more than strength — a mild acid used consistently beats a strong one that forces you to stop.
