Hydroquinone and the Korean brightening shelf: two different worlds

⏱ 2 min read

Why you will not find it on a Korean shelf

Korea regulates brightening claims through the 기능성화장품 (gineungseong hwajangpum, 'functional cosmetics') system, and the approved 미백 (mibaek, 'brightening') actives are a defined list — niacinamide and arbutin among the familiar ones. Hydroquinone is not on it. Anything sold to you as a Korean cream containing hydroquinone is either mislabelled or not a cosmetic, and creams bought informally overseas have repeatedly turned out to contain undeclared ingredients. If a clinic in Korea dispenses something for pigmentation, it is dispensing it as a clinic, with a consultation attached.

Why courses are managed rather than open-ended

Prescribers generally treat hydroquinone as something used in a planned way rather than indefinitely, partly because of irritation and partly because of exogenous ochronosis, an uncommon but reported darkening associated with prolonged use. That is precisely why it is not a shelf product. Anyone offering you an unlimited supply without follow-up is doing you no favours. If a pigmentation plan involves it, the review appointments are part of the plan, not an optional extra, and any decision to continue, pause or switch is the clinician's.

What the Korean routine can genuinely add

Sunscreen, relentlessly, because unprotected UV works against every pigmentation approach that exists — and Korea's sunscreen category is the most usable in the world, which makes daily reapplication realistic instead of aspirational. Beyond that: niacinamide, arbutin, tranexamic-type ingredients and vitamin C are the cosmetic supporting cast, and they are cosmetic. They influence the look of tone gradually. Whether any of them belong on top of a prescription, and in what order, is worth asking rather than assuming, because irritation on treated skin can leave marks of its own.

The melasma reality check

If the pigmentation in question is melasma, temper the marketing. It is widely described as recurring, sun- and heat-sensitive, and stubborn even under specialist care, and no Korean essence changes that. Heat matters as well as light — a 찜질방 (jjimjilbang, Korean bathhouse) session, a hot yoga class or a steaming commute can undo careful work. The realistic frame is long-term management by a clinician with cosmetic support around it, not a course of anything that finishes. Anyone promising to clear it permanently is selling something.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a Korean toner and how is it different?

In Korea, toner (called 'skin' or 'skin toner') is usually a gentle, hydrating step that preps and balances skin after cleansing, rather than a harsh astringent. It's meant to add moisture and help following layers absorb, not to strip or tighten the skin.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.
