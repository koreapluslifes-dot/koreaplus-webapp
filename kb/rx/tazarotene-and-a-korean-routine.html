Tazarotene: the strictest retinoid to build a routine around

⏱ 3 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Tazarotene is prescription-only and generally considered the most irritating of the common topical retinoids, so the routine around it is usually stripped further back than it would be for tretinoin or adapalene. Tazarotene is prescription-only and generally considered the most irritating of the common topical retinoids, so the routine around it is usually stripped further back than it would be for tretinoin or adapalene. Expect a clinician to want a bland cleanser, a plain moisturiser and daily sunscreen, and to want everything that exfoliates, tingles or fragrances removed. Every decision about how and when it is applied belongs to the prescriber, including any decision to change it.

Why the routine gets smaller than usual

The stronger the retinoid, the less room there is for anything else, and tazarotene sits at the demanding end. Dryness, stinging on application, visible peeling and a temporarily rough surface are commonly reported, which means the supporting products have one job: keep the skin comfortable enough that you continue. This is not the moment for a ten-step routine, a new essence you saw recommended, or a 각질 (gakjil) removing gel. If a product does anything other than cleanse, hydrate, moisturise or block UV, assume it is out until you have asked.

Sensation is not a progress meter

A persistent belief in beauty forums is that a strong reaction means it is working. It does not follow. Irritation and the changes a retinoid is prescribed for are not the same process, and a face too sore to keep treating has stopped delivering anything at all. Korean skincare is unusually well equipped for the comfort side — 저자극 (jeojageuk, 'low-irritation') lines, unscented barrier creams, thick sleeping masks used simply as an overnight occlusive. Use them for that, and let the prescriber judge whether the reaction you are having is within the expected range.

Pregnancy, planning and the conversation to have early

Topical retinoids as a class are treated cautiously in pregnancy, and tazarotene is handled with particular strictness by prescribers. This is not a decision to work out from an article or a forum thread. If you are pregnant, trying to conceive or considering it, that belongs at the top of the conversation with the clinician who prescribed it and with whoever manages your pregnancy care — before anything changes, not after. The same applies to anyone else's prescription: it is theirs, and sharing it is a genuinely bad idea.

Sun protection carries more weight here

Skin treated with a strong retinoid is commonly described as more sun-sensitive, and the surface is often flaking, which makes an even sunscreen layer harder to achieve. Korean sun fluids and light gels tend to sit better over flaking than heavy creams, and a hat plus shade does work no formula can. Reapplication over a dry, patchy surface is the practical problem people underestimate — a cushion or stick over a fluid is one common workaround, and the honest answer is that it protects less well than a fresh full layer.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.

How often should you exfoliate in a Korean routine?

Once or twice a week for most people, up to three times for oily and resilient skin. Frequency matters far more than strength — a mild acid used consistently beats a strong one that forces you to stop.
