What a personal colour consultation actually gives you, and what it does not

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: A personal colour (퍼스널 컬러, peoseuneol keolleo) consultation is a draping session: a consultant holds fabric or paper swatches beneath your face under controlled light and judges which ones make your skin look even and awake. It works well as a shopping filter and it is an enjoyable hour. It is also a professional convention rather than a validated science, and two consultants can hand you two different seasons.

What happens in the room

You arrive with a bare face and your hair covered, and you sit under standardised lighting or beside a north-facing window. The consultant drapes contrasting swatches under your chin — warm against cool first, then variations in depth and clarity — and watches how your skin, the shadows beneath your eyes and your lips respond to each. You are shown the comparisons directly, which is the persuasive part of the experience. Sessions usually end with a palette card, often lipstick swatches tried on you, and recommendations for clothing and makeup shades.

Why it can be worth doing

The value is structural rather than mystical. Korean cosmetics offer an enormous number of near-identical shades, and any principled filter cuts down expensive guessing. Draping also forces you to look at your own face under consistent light while somebody changes one variable at a time, which almost nobody manages alone in a shop lit by warm bulbs. People commonly come away buying less and wearing more of what they already own. That outcome is real even if the framework generating it is soft.

Where the framework is soft

Seasonal colour analysis descends from twentieth-century Western colour-theory traditions, and the four-, twelve- and sixteen-season expansions are conventions rather than findings. There is no standard qualification, no calibration between consultants, and no controlled evidence that a person has one objectively correct palette. Re-testing with a different consultant, different lighting or a tan can move your result. Anyone presenting your season as a fixed biological fact is overselling it, and the habit of ranking some seasons as more desirable than others deserves no respect whatsoever.

Using the result sensibly

Treat the palette as a shortlist rather than a rulebook. It is a good answer to which of six red lipsticks to try first, and a poor reason to give away a coat you like. Hold products against your jaw in daylight before buying, because a swatch the theory approves of can still be wrong on your face. And if a colour makes you happy, wear it — the consultation was only ever about what flatters, which is one of several reasons anybody chooses what they wear.
