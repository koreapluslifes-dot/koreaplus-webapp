Cushion foundation (쿠션): what is inside the compact and why it behaves that way

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: A cushion compact is liquid base held inside a saturated sponge and dispensed by pressing an applicator puff against it. The sponge meters the dose, so each press delivers a thin, buildable layer with a wetter finish than a bottled foundation gives. Because the formula sits open to air and meets a used puff repeatedly, it is more heavily preserved and shorter-lived than most base products.

What the sponge is doing

A cushion (쿠션, kusyeon) is a liquid base held inside an open-cell foam pad — usually polyurethane or a rubber-type sponge — sitting under a lid with an applicator puff. The foam is a metering device. Press the puff into it and capillary action releases a small, fairly consistent amount rather than the puddle you get from tipping a bottle. That is why cushion technique is pressing and rolling rather than sweeping: you are transferring thin films from puff to skin, several times over, instead of spreading one thick layer. Coverage is built by repetition, not by pressure.

Why the finish looks wetter

Cushion formulas generally run higher in water and volatile silicone and lower in pigment than a bottled foundation from the same brand, which is what produces the fresh, slightly damp finish and the tendency to look thin over dry patches. Many include film formers so that water phase does not simply sit on the surface, plus UV filters, since most Korean cushions carry an SPF claim. The wet look is partly formula and partly timing — a cushion looks its best in the first few minutes, before the volatiles flash off and the film settles down onto the skin.

The puff is part of the product

Performance is split between the formula and the applicator, which is why swapping in a random sponge changes the result. Air-cushion puffs are dense and low-absorbency, so they hand most of the product to the skin; a damp beauty sponge drinks some of it and sheers the layer out. Press with the flat of the puff across the centre of the face, then use a folded edge around the nose and under the eyes where the surface is uneven. Let each pass dry for a few seconds before adding another, or the second layer lifts the first straight off.

Where cushions fall down

Two honest limits are worth stating. The SPF figure is measured at an application density of 2 mg/cm², a visibly thick layer that nobody achieves by pressing a puff over their face, so treat a cushion as a bonus on top of sunscreen rather than as a replacement for it. And the widely repeated claim that cushions oxidise and turn orange is often something simpler: a thin, wet film looks lighter than it will once it has set and settled against your own tone. Judge a shade twenty minutes after application, in daylight, before blaming chemistry.
