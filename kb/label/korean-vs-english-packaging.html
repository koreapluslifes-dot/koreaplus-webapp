Why the Korean and export versions of a product differ

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Export versions are frequently reformulated for the destination market's regulations, which may restrict filters, preservatives or actives that Korea permits. Labels are also translated and sometimes simplified, so a claim or marking present on the Korean box may be absent on the English one — the same product name does not guarantee the same formula.

Regulations force reformulation

Korea permits a range of modern organic filters the United States does not. That gap narrowed in 2026, when the FDA cleared bemotrizinol — Tinosorb S — for US sunscreens, the first new filter approved there in more than two decades. Others Korea uses freely, such as bisoctrizole (Tinosorb M) and diethylamino hydroxybenzoyl hexyl benzoate (Uvinul A Plus), still are not permitted in the US. Preservative rules and permitted concentrations differ too. Where they conflict, the export version is reformulated — the same name, a different product.

Claims get translated away

A 기능성화장품 marking has no meaning outside Korea, so it is often dropped. Brightening claims are frequently reworded for markets where such language is regulated differently. The product may be identical while the box says much less.

How to tell which you have

Look for full Korean text with the Korean regulatory markings, versus a multilingual label listing an importer and local compliance information. The presence of a local importer address usually means an export version.

Does it matter?

For sunscreen, yes — the filter list is the product. For most other categories the differences are minor. If you specifically want the Korean formula, buy through Korean channels and check the ingredient list rather than the name.
