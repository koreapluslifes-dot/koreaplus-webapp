Fractional Lasers Explained: What 'Fractional', 'Ablative' and 'Non-Ablative' Mean

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: 'Fractional' means the laser acts on skin in a grid of microscopic columns and leaves untouched skin in between, instead of covering the whole surface at once. Ablative devices remove tissue within those columns; non-ablative ones deposit heat below the surface without removing it, which generally trades intensity for an easier recovery. These are medical procedures with genuine risks, and only a licensed doctor who has examined your skin can advise on whether any of it applies to you.

What 'fractional' actually means

Picture a grid stamped across the skin. The device acts only at the grid points, leaving the surrounding skin untouched, and the body responds to those small controlled columns over the following days and weeks. The reasoning is straightforward: treating a fraction of the area at a time is generally less demanding than treating all of it. That is the whole idea behind the word, and it applies across very different machines. Two clinics can both offer something 'fractional' and be using devices with little else in common.

The ablative and non-ablative trade

Ablative devices vaporise tissue in each column; non-ablative devices heat tissue while leaving the surface intact. The trade-off is consistent and unglamorous: the more intense the intervention, the more disruptive the recovery period tends to be, and the gentler the setting, the more modest and gradual any visible change. Be sceptical of marketing that promises both ends at once. Where a clinic claims dramatic change with no meaningful recovery, ask what settings produce that combination, and treat the answer as information about the clinic.

Risks that get skipped in the brochure

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the recurring concern in richly pigmented skin, including many East Asian skin types, and it can outlast the original reason for the procedure. Infection, prolonged redness, textural change, reactivation of cold sores and, uncommonly, scarring all appear in the literature. Recovery time is not a fixed figure: it depends on device, settings, area, and the person, so any single number quoted online should be treated as one person's experience rather than a rule. Your clinic's own written aftercare is the relevant guidance.

The questions worth asking a clinician

Useful consultation questions are specific: which device, ablative or not, what settings and why those, what has been seen in skin like yours, what the realistic recovery pattern looks like for the area being discussed, and what the plan is if something goes wrong. A clinic that answers those clearly is telling you something. This page describes categories only — it recommends nothing, states no criteria for who is or is not suitable, and cannot substitute for a licensed doctor assessing you face to face.
