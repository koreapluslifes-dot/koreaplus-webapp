The roadshop boom and bust: how single-brand street stores rose and fell

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: 로드샵 (rodeu-syap) refers to the single-brand street stores that defined Korean beauty retail through the 2000s and into the mid-2010s, where every shopfront on a strip sold one brand and staff handed out free sheet masks to pull passers-by inside. 로드샵 (rodeu-syap) refers to the single-brand street stores that defined Korean beauty retail through the 2000s and into the mid-2010s, where every shopfront on a strip sold one brand and staff handed out free sheet masks to pull passers-by inside. The format was undone by multi-brand chains, online shopping, a sharp late-2010s fall in Chinese group tourism and then the pandemic. Most of the brands survived in some form; the store estates did not.

What the boom looked like

Myeongdong was the emblem: adjacent shopfronts running Etude House, Missha, The Face Shop, Nature Republic, Innisfree, Tony Moly, Skinfood and Holika Holika within a couple of blocks. The mechanics were loud — one-plus-one baskets, a free mask with any purchase, staff calling from doorways in Korean, Chinese and Japanese, and a launch cadence fast enough that the shelf looked different month to month. The same format replicated in university districts and neighbourhood high streets across the country, usually as franchises.

Why it worked while it worked

Vertical control was the advantage. One brand owned the shelf, the price, the staff and the story, so a trend item could go from decision to street quickly and the margin was legible. Group tourism supplied volume that domestic demand alone would not have produced, particularly in central Seoul. The lasting cultural effect was a repricing of the whole category: skincare stopped being something bought from a salesperson at a counter and became something you picked up on the way home.

What broke it

Multi-brand health-and-beauty chains let a shopper compare brands in one trip, which a single-brand store structurally cannot answer. Online undercut store prices on identical stock. The late-2010s downturn in Chinese group tourism removed the volume that prime-district rents were underwritten by, and the pandemic removed most of what was left. Rents in central shopping districts fall much more slowly than footfall does, so the squeeze arrived from both sides at once and closures ran through franchisees as well as company-operated stores.

Where those brands are now

Most pivoted rather than disappeared: far fewer directly operated stores, shelf space inside multi-brand chains, their own online stores, and export through overseas retailers and marketplaces. Some contracted severely, and at least one well-known name went through court-led restructuring in the late 2010s. The products themselves largely still exist and several remain popular abroad. If you are shopping for them in Korea today, expect to find them on a chain shelf or a website rather than in a shop devoted to them.
