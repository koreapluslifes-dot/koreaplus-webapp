Military service and men's skincare: what conscription actually does to a routine

What service does to a routine

Able-bodied Korean men serve for a period that varies by branch, typically somewhere around eighteen months. The conditions do the work: long days outdoors, sweat under equipment, chinstraps and helmets, wind and dry winter air, communal washrooms and early starts. Product access narrows to what the on-base shop stocks or what arrives in a parcel from home. Routines shrink accordingly, and what survives is whatever absorbs quickly, needs no mirror and does not announce itself by scent. Most men leave with a simpler routine than they had going in, and many keep it.

Sun exposure is the real variable

Field training happens outdoors in whatever weather exists, and reapplying sunscreen partway through an exercise is rarely realistic. That makes cumulative ultraviolet exposure the one genuinely defensible skin concern of this period, well ahead of anything about pores or texture. Hats, sleeves and shade do more practical work here than any product. It is worth being blunt about the sequence: protection during the exposure is what counts, and nothing bought afterwards reverses accumulated sun damage, whatever the packaging on a post-discharge gift set implies.

Discharge as a reset point

전역 (jeonyeok), discharge, is a marked event. Hair grows back, appearance re-enters the picture, and a fair amount of buying happens around it — often as gifts, since sets given before enlistment and after discharge are a small genre of their own. The effect on the wider culture is less about spending than visibility: a very large share of Korean men have shared the same grooming baseline at the same age, which is part of why men buying skincare here reads as ordinary rather than as a statement.

The claim to be sceptical about

The tidy story is that conscription manufactured a generation of male skincare buyers. It is repeated often and it is not testable. Idol and actor visibility, appearance expectations in hiring and customer-facing work, and the simple fact that men's products are stocked everywhere all push the same way, and no one can separate their contributions from outside the market. Take the correlation, leave the causation. One practical note: breakouts driven by friction, heat or occlusion under equipment are common during service and are a clinician's question, not a routine-building exercise.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.

Is Korean skincare different for men?

Skin is skin — the actives that work do not change by gender. What differs is context: shaving irritates and mildly exfoliates, male skin tends to be thicker and oilier on average, and men's ranges are usually formulated lighter and unscented.
