Escape the corset: the argument Korea is still having about beauty standards

⏱ 3 min read
⚡ Quick answer: 탈코르셋 (tal-koreuset), usually translated as escape the corset, is a South Korean movement in which mostly younger women publicly gave up makeup, long hair and other appearance routines they described as compulsory rather than chosen. It grew out of the feminist wave of the late 2010s and drew strong support and strong criticism inside Korea, often from women on both sides. It is a live argument about whether beauty work is freely chosen, not a settled verdict.

탈코르셋 (tal-koreuset), usually translated as escape the corset, is a South Korean movement in which mostly younger women publicly gave up makeup, long hair and other appearance routines they described as compulsory rather than chosen. It grew out of the feminist wave of the late 2010s and drew strong support and strong criticism inside Korea, often from women on both sides. It is a live argument about whether beauty work is freely chosen, not a settled verdict.

What the movement actually involved

Participants cut their hair short, stopped wearing makeup, swapped contact lenses for glasses and changed how they dressed. Some posted videos of cosmetics being destroyed, which is the image that travelled furthest abroad and was never representative of most participants. The framing was practical as much as ideological: the money, the morning time and the mental attention that a full appearance routine consumes. Some described it as a private decision to stop; others treated it as collective political action with a visible uniform. Online communities and video platforms carried most of it, which shaped how confrontational it looked from outside.

The case its participants made

The core argument was that appearance in Korea functions less like a preference and more like an entry requirement — for hiring, for service work, for being taken seriously — and that a choice made under that pressure is not obviously free. Supporters pointed to the density of the beauty industry, the historical norm of photographs on job applications, and the routine commentary women received about their faces at school and at work. They also pointed to distribution: the labour, cost and risk of falling short landed overwhelmingly on women. Opting out, on that reading, carries a cost precisely because the standard is not optional.

The case against it, made inside Korea

Critics, many of them women who identify as feminists, argued that grooming can be craft, pleasure or self-expression, and that treating makeup as false consciousness dismisses the people wearing it. A second objection was tactical: online shaming of women who kept wearing makeup reproduced the policing it set out to oppose. A third was structural — asking individuals to exit a norm puts the burden on them rather than on employers, broadcasters or schools that set the norm in the first place. Coverage abroad frequently flattened all of this into a single tidy narrative it does not fit.

What it did and did not change

It changed vocabulary. The term entered ordinary conversation, appearance-based comments became easier to name as a problem, and some marketing shifted towards language about choice rather than obligation. What it did to spending is genuinely unclear, and worth treating with suspicion whenever you see a number attached. Domestic cosmetics sales over the same years were moved far more visibly by tourism, exports and then the pandemic, and untangling one influence from the others is not something anyone can do credibly from the outside. Norms move slowly. The argument has not concluded; it has become ordinary.
