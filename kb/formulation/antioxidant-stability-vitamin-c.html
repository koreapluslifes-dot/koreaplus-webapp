Antioxidant Stability: Why Vitamin C Is So Hard to Formulate

⚡ Quick answer: Pure L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is a powerful antioxidant but unstable: it oxidizes with air, light, heat, and water, turning yellow-brown and losing punch. Formulators fight this with low pH below 3.5, useful concentrations around 10–20%, antioxidant partners, and protective packaging.

Pure L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is a powerful antioxidant but unstable: it oxidizes with air, light, heat, and water, turning yellow-brown and losing punch. Formulators fight this with low pH below 3.5, useful concentrations around 10–20%, antioxidant partners, and protective packaging.

Why vitamin C degrades

L-ascorbic acid readily reacts with oxygen, especially in water and when exposed to light and heat. As it oxidizes it discolors toward yellow or brown and becomes less effective. A bottle that has turned deep amber has likely lost much of its antioxidant value.

How formulators stabilize it

Effective L-ascorbic acid serums are typically formulated at pH below 3.5 and at roughly 10–20%, the well-established cosmetic range. Antioxidant companions like vitamin E and ferulic acid help, anhydrous or low-water formats slow oxidation, and opaque, air-limiting packaging protects the contents. Derivatives such as ascorbyl glucoside are more stable and gentler, though generally milder.

Using it well

Vitamin C is best applied in the morning under sunscreen for antioxidant defense and a brighter, more even look. Store it cool and capped, and replace it once it darkens. These are cosmetic, look-focused benefits, supporting tone and radiance, not a medical treatment.
