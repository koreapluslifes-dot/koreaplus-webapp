Spotting counterfeit cosmetics at market stalls and informal resellers

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Counterfeit cosmetics in Korea turn up overwhelmingly through unofficial resellers — tourist stalls, pop-up tables and open marketplaces — rather than in chains, department stores or brand shops. The reliable signals are a price far below every legitimate channel, missing batch and manufacture codes, absent Korean-language ingredient labelling, and print that is subtly wrong. The simplest defence is unglamorous: buy through official channels.

Where the risk sits

Chains, department-store counters, brand stores, duty-free and brand-operated online shops are supplied directly and are effectively a non-issue. Risk concentrates wherever provenance is unclear: unattributed stalls, informal resellers, secondhand sales and marketplace sellers with no trading history. Most such sellers are handling genuine stock. The problem is that you have no way to distinguish the ones who are not, and no recourse at all if the item turns out to be fake once you have flown home.

What to check on the box

Cosmetics sold legitimately in Korea carry Korean-language labelling with the ingredient list, the manufacturer or importer, and dates. A box carrying none of that was not intended for Korean retail, whatever the seller says. Look for a batch or lot code, and for 제조 (jejo) and 사용기한 (sayonggihan) printed on the packaging. Then look at the printing itself: misaligned text, substituted fonts, poor colour registration, a cellophane wrap that is loose or unevenly sealed. Counterfeits usually fail on print before they fail on formula.

Tells that are not reliable

Smell, texture and colour are weak evidence. Brands reformulate regularly, so a product differing from the version you remember has probably been reworked rather than faked. Holograms, seals and security stickers are copied routinely. A scannable QR code proves almost nothing, since the code can be lifted from a genuine package. Treat sensory impressions as a prompt to check provenance, never as a conclusion — the question that actually decides it is where the item came from.

Testers, not-for-sale units and loose bundles

Units marked 비매품 (bimaepum, not for sale) or 견본 (gyeonbon, sample) were never meant to be sold, frequently carry no traceable date, and may have been stored anywhere for any length of time. Loose sheet masks bagged in tens with no outer packaging are impossible to verify at all. If you have used something of unknown origin and your skin reacts, stop and see a doctor or pharmacist — an unidentified product is not something to reason your way through alone.
