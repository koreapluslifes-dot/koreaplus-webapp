Chronic hand eczema: wet work, soap and a barrier that never gets a break

⏱ 2 min read

Hand eczema is persistent inflammation of the skin of the hands — dryness, cracking, itching, sometimes crops of small deep blisters — and it is strongly associated with repeated wet work, soaps, solvents and glove occlusion, which is why hairdressers, nurses, cleaners and cooks get it disproportionately. It is a medical diagnosis, and cracks that bleed or weep need a clinician. Around that care, relentless emollient use and glove discipline do most of the practical work.

Why hands are the hardest case

Palm and finger skin is thick, so it fissures rather than flaking, and fissures are painful and slow. Hands are also washed constantly, exposed to detergents, food acids, solvents and metals, and then sealed inside gloves that trap sweat. There is never a recovery window. Add nail involvement, and the everyday reality is that any barrier repair achieved overnight is undone by nine in the morning. This is not a hygiene failure — if anything, frequent washing is part of the mechanism.

The occupational and allergic layers

Two distinct things often sit on top of each other. Irritant damage from repeated wet work and detergents accumulates in almost anyone with enough exposure. Allergic contact dermatitis to a specific substance — rubber accelerators in gloves, preservatives, hair-product ingredients, metals, certain foods handled raw — can hide inside the same picture and will not improve until it is identified. That is what patch testing is for, and it is one of the strongest arguments for a dermatology referral rather than another hand cream.

The routine that actually helps

Emollient after every single hand wash, and again before bed — carry a tube, keep one at the sink. Bland and fragrance-free, thicker than feels elegant; ointments overnight under cotton gloves are unglamorous and effective. Lukewarm water, a mild cleanser, dry thoroughly including between fingers. Cotton liners inside waterproof gloves for wet tasks, and change them when damp. Alcohol sanitiser stings cracked skin badly and is not a substitute for the routine. Rings trap detergent, so remove them before washing up.

When to be seen

Cracks that bleed or weep, yellow crusting, spreading redness, or increasing pain suggest infection and need prompt attention. So does eczema that is not improving with consistent emollient use, that is interfering with work, or that keeps recurring seasonally. If it is work-related, say so at the appointment — occupational health measures and a formal record can matter for both the skin and the job. Cosmetic hand creams help comfort; they do not treat this, and no cream on a shelf claims to.
