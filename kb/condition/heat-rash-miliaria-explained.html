Heat rash: what 땀띠 actually is, and why cooling beats creams

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Heat rash is what happens when sweat ducts become blocked and sweat leaks into the skin instead of reaching the surface, producing tiny clear blisters or itchy prickly bumps in covered, sweaty areas. Cooling the skin and stopping the sweating is what resolves it; occlusive creams and powders often prolong it by blocking further. If it becomes painful or pustular, or spreads with fever, see a doctor, since infected sweat glands are a genuine complication.

What is actually blocked

땀띠 (ttamtti) is a plumbing problem, not an allergy. When sweat cannot escape, it accumulates and leaks into surrounding tissue. If the blockage is very superficial, the result is tiny clear fragile blisters that break at a touch and barely itch. If it sits deeper, the result is the red prickly itchy bumps most people mean by heat rash. It concentrates where clothing traps heat and where skin rubs — the back, chest, waistband, under the breasts, neck folds, and anywhere a strap sits.

The Korean summer version

The 장마 (jangma) monsoon and the humid weeks that follow are a near-perfect setup: high humidity means sweat evaporates poorly, and long commutes in heat compound it. This is exactly the season when a rich occlusive night cream, heavy body oil or thick sunscreen layered over the chest and back stops helping and starts trapping. Switching to lighter gel-textured hydration through the humid months is not a marketing gimmick; it is a sensible response to the way sweat behaves in that weather.

What actually helps

Get cool and stay dry. Air conditioning or a fan, loose breathable fabrics, cool showers, and drying skin properly rather than dressing while damp. Remove the occlusion — that means pausing heavy creams, thick balms and body oils over affected areas, and reconsidering talc-style powders, which cake and can add to the blockage. Calamine is widely used for the itch and is a comfort measure, not a fix. Do not scrub. Most cases settle within days once the skin stops sweating into itself.

When it is not heat rash

See a doctor if bumps become painful, filled with pus, or surrounded by spreading warm redness, if there is fever, or if the rash persists after several cool dry days. Blocked sweat glands can become infected, and deeper forms can affect the body's ability to cool itself, which matters in serious heat. It is also readily confused with fungal folliculitis, an allergic reaction or an irritant rash from a new product, and those go in different directions — a clinician sorts that out.
