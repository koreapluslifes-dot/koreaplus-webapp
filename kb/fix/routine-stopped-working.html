Your routine stopped working: what usually changed, and what did not

Skin rarely becomes immune to skincare. Far more often the season changed, the product oxidised, the formula was quietly reformulated, your application drifted, or your baseline shifted so the improvement you were getting no longer registers as improvement. The tolerance story — that skin adapts and you must rotate products — is repeated far more confidently than the evidence for cosmetic ingredients supports.

The tolerance claim, examined

Tachyphylaxis, a diminishing response with repeated exposure, is real in some areas of pharmacology and gets borrowed enthusiastically by beauty marketing to justify buying a new product. For the ingredients in most cosmetics, applied topically at cosmetic concentrations, the case is thin. Niacinamide does not stop working because it got bored. What is far more likely is that something else in the picture moved, and rotating your entire routine on the assumption of tolerance means you lose the one thing that was actually helping.

The things that usually did change

Seasons: a cream that suited December is oppressive in July, and a gel that suited July leaves you flaking in December. Product age: antioxidant serums in particular degrade, and a browning vitamin C is no longer the product you bought. Reformulation happens quietly and ingredient lists change between batches. And adherence drifts — most people apply markedly less sunscreen and markedly less of everything in month six than in week one, without noticing at all.

Your baseline moved with you

This one is uncomfortable but common. A routine that visibly improved things did so against a starting point you no longer remember accurately. Six months later you are comparing today's skin with today's expectations rather than with where you began, and the product looks like a failure. It is the same effect that makes a room's new paint invisible after a fortnight. Photographs taken in the same light at the same time of day are the only real defence, and almost nobody takes them.

How to test it properly

Do not replace everything at once, which destroys the information. Change one variable, hold it for at least four to six weeks for anything affecting texture or pigmentation, and photograph consistently. Check dates and storage on every bottle first — that alone resolves a fair number of cases. If your goal is one that cosmetics were never going to reach, such as substantially changing established pigmentation or deep lines, then the routine did not stop working. It reached its ceiling, and that conversation belongs with a dermatologist.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.

How often should you exfoliate in a Korean routine?

Once or twice a week for most people, up to three times for oily and resilient skin. Frequency matters far more than strength — a mild acid used consistently beats a strong one that forces you to stop.
