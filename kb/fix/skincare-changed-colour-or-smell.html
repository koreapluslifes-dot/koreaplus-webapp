Your product changed colour or smell: what it means and when to bin it

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: A change in colour, smell or texture means the formula has altered since you bought it, usually through oxidation, heat, light or contamination from fingers and air. Vitamin C serums turning yellow then amber then brown are the classic case. Colour change does not always mean a product is unsafe, but it does mean it is no longer the product you tested — and a rancid or sour smell is a straightforward reason to throw it away.

A change in colour, smell or texture means the formula has altered since you bought it, usually through oxidation, heat, light or contamination from fingers and air. Vitamin C serums turning yellow then amber then brown are the classic case. Colour change does not always mean a product is unsafe, but it does mean it is no longer the product you tested — and a rancid or sour smell is a straightforward reason to throw it away.

Oxidation, the most common cause

Antioxidants work by being oxidised, which means they are consumed over time by exposure to air, light and warmth. L-ascorbic acid serums are the clearest example: a fresh one is near-colourless, then goes pale yellow, then amber, then brown. Some darkening is normal and does not mean the product is dangerous, but a deeply brown serum has lost much of what you were paying for. Plant extracts and unrefined oils darken too. Opaque, airtight packaging slows all of this considerably, which is why so many Korean serums arrive in dark pumps rather than droppers.

What smell is telling you

Fragrance-free products are the most informative, because there is nothing masking the change. A faintly sour, crayon-like or old-nut smell in an oil or an oil-rich cream suggests the lipids have gone rancid. A sharp or yeasty note in a water-based product suggests microbial growth, which matters most in jars, in anything you dip fingers into, and in anything sold as preservative-free. Trust your nose here. A product that smells wrong is not one to test on your face to see what happens.

Where you keep it matters more than you think

Bathrooms are warm, humid and bright, which is close to the worst possible storage for actives, and they are where almost everyone keeps everything. A bedroom drawer is better. Sunlight on a windowsill is worse still. Cars in summer are worst of all. Refrigeration helps vitamin C and some oils but is unnecessary for most products and can make thick creams unpleasant to spread. Close lids properly, use a spatula for jars, and do not decant into unsterile containers for travel.

The rules for throwing things away

Check the period-after-opening symbol on the packaging — a small open jar marked 6M, 12M and so on — and write the opening date on the bottle in marker, since nobody remembers. Bin anything that has separated and will not remix, anything that smells sour or rancid, anything with visible growth, and anything whose texture has changed markedly. If a product causes stinging it never used to cause, treat that as a further reason to discard rather than a puzzle to solve. Sunscreen past its date should be replaced without deliberation.
