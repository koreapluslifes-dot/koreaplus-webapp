Winter skin: why the same routine stops being enough in December

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Cold air holds very little moisture, indoor heating dries it further, and the gap between the two drives water out of the skin surface faster than in summer. The routine has not failed — the conditions changed. Richer textures, gentler cleansing, cooler showers, less frequent exfoliation and a humidifier address most of it. Korean winters, cold and notably dry, are why the local market swings so hard toward heavy creams from November.

What the season is actually doing

Cold air carries far less water vapour than warm air, and heating it indoors drops the relative humidity further. Skin loses water to that dry air continuously, and the surface lipids that slow the loss become less pliable at low temperatures. Add wind, which strips the surface mechanically, and hot showers, which strip it chemically, and a routine that was comfortably sufficient in September falls short. Nothing about your skin has changed. The environment did, and the routine needs to answer it.

Adjusting the routine, in order of impact

Move to a richer moisturiser or add an occlusive step at night. Switch a foaming cleanser for a cream or oil-based one and cut morning cleansing to water alone if your skin is tight. Apply humectants onto damp skin, since glycerin and hyaluronic acid draw water from wherever they can, and in dry air that can mean from your own skin if there is nothing sealing them. Cut exfoliation frequency roughly in half. Lower your shower temperature, which nobody wants to hear in January.

The Korean winter shelf

Seoul winters are cold and dry, and the local market reflects it: heavy 크림 (keurim) textures, sleeping masks used as an overnight seal, oil-based cleansers, ceramide-forward barrier creams and a general seasonal pivot in what shops put at the front. Ampoules with panthenol and centella-derived ingredients are marketed hard for 진정 (jinjeong), calming, in this period. The seasonal logic is sound even where individual claims are inflated — the useful part is simply that most people should not run the same routine year round.

Where winter dryness becomes medical

Flaking that cracks or bleeds, patches that itch persistently, redness with scaling in the eyebrows or around the nose, or dryness so severe that a rich moisturiser gives no relief at all — these are not seasonal inconvenience. Eczema, seborrhoeic dermatitis and other conditions flare in winter and they are diagnosed and treated medically. If a genuinely bland, rich routine and a humidifier have not helped after a few weeks, book an appointment rather than buying a fifth cream.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.

How often should you exfoliate in a Korean routine?

Once or twice a week for most people, up to three times for oily and resilient skin. Frequency matters far more than strength — a mild acid used consistently beats a strong one that forces you to stop.
