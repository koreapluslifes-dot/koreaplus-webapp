Slugging gone wrong: when an occlusive layer leaves you congested

⏱ 2 min read

Occlusives such as petrolatum are, on their own, among the least likely ingredients to block pores. What they do is amplify. Sealing an unwashed face, an active-heavy routine or a rich cream under an impermeable layer for eight hours magnifies whatever is underneath, and heat and sweat under that layer add to it. If slugging congests you, look at what you sealed in, not just what you sealed with.

The occlusive is rarely the culprit

Petrolatum is a large, inert molecule that sits on the surface and slows water loss. It is one of the most studied and least reactive materials in the whole category, widely used in medical settings for exactly that reason. Blaming it for congestion is usually blaming the wrong layer. The Korean and Western enthusiasm for 슬러깅 (seulleoging), slugging, has produced a lot of confident claims in both directions, and the honest position is that the occlusive itself does very little except stop things leaving.

What an occlusive amplifies

Anything already on your face stays there, in contact, for the whole night. That includes residual sunscreen if your evening cleanse was casual, the emollients in a rich cream, sweat if your bedroom is warm, and whatever transfers from a pillowcase you have been sleeping on all week. It also holds actives against skin for far longer than intended, which is why sealing a retinoid or an acid under petrolatum is a reliable way to overshoot. The layer is neutral. The contents are not.

How to keep the benefit without the congestion

Cleanse thoroughly first — a proper double cleanse, 이중세안 (ijung sean), on any day you wore sunscreen or makeup. Seal over something simple: a plain humectant or a light moisturiser, never a heavy occlusive over a heavy cream. Keep actives out of the sandwich entirely, or use them on separate nights. Apply a thin film, not a mask. Try it on two or three nights a week rather than every night, and skip it in humid weather when your skin is losing very little water anyway. Change pillowcases more often than feels necessary.

Who should probably not slug

If you are prone to inflammatory acne or to small clustered bumps along the jaw and hairline, an eight-hour occlusive layer is a poor bet even done carefully. The same applies if you run oily in summer. Slugging suits dry, tight, flaky skin in cold dry air, and that is where the enthusiasm comes from. It is not a universal upgrade, and treating it as one is how a technique that helps one skin type acquires a reputation for congesting everyone else.
