When to stop troubleshooting your routine and see a doctor

⏱ 2 min read
⚡ Quick answer: Stop experimenting and book an appointment if something is painful, spreading, blistering, weeping or swelling; if acne is cystic or leaving marks; if a rash keeps returning across unrelated products; if a patch has persisted for weeks despite a bland routine; or if a mole has changed. The urgent list

Swelling of the lips, tongue or eyelids, difficulty breathing, or a rash spreading over the body needs emergency care, not a forum. So does sudden one-sided facial swelling with pain, any rapid change in vision, or signs of infection — spreading heat, yellow crusting, fever. Blistering, weeping or severe pain after a peel or a strong active also warrants same-day attention. None of these are situations where waiting to see whether a barrier cream helps is a reasonable plan, and the cost of being cautious here is very low.

Things that are diagnoses, not skin types

Rosacea, eczema, seborrhoeic dermatitis, perioral dermatitis, psoriasis, melasma, fungal folliculitis and contact allergy are all commonly self-labelled as sensitive skin, dryness, congestion or stubborn pigmentation. Several of them are made worse by exactly the products people reach for. Perioral dermatitis in particular is often aggravated by heavy creams and by steroid creams borrowed from a drawer. If your skin problem has a shape, a location and a persistence to it, the useful question is what it is, and that requires someone who can examine you.

The slower signals people ignore

Acne that is cystic, painful or leaving marks and scars deserves medical treatment now rather than after another six months of trials, because the marks are the part that is hard to undo. A patch that has not shifted in six weeks on a genuinely bland routine is not a routine problem. Reactions to multiple unrelated products point to a shared allergen and need proper patch testing. Hair loss, persistent itching, and any new, changing, bleeding or irregular mole all belong with a doctor promptly.

Going in prepared

Bring photographs of the problem at its worst, since skin has a habit of behaving in waiting rooms. Bring a list of every product you use, including hair products, and the packaging of anything you suspect — ingredient lists are genuinely useful to a dermatologist. Note when it started, what makes it better or worse, and what you have already tried and for how long. In Korea, dermatology clinics — 피부과 (pibugwa) — are widely accessible, but wherever you are, going earlier costs less than going later.

What is the 10-step Korean skincare routine?

The 10-step Korean skincare routine is a layered framework popularized in Korea: oil cleanser, water cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum or ampoule, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturizer and sunscreen. It's a customizable menu, not a daily mandate, and most people use only the steps their skin needs.

What is a good Korean skincare routine for beginners?

A simple beginner Korean routine is a gentle cleanser, a hydrating toner, a lightweight moisturizer and daily sunscreen, adding one targeted serum once your skin adjusts. Introduce actives slowly, one at a time, and patch-test, following the Korean principle of gentle, barrier-friendly care over many steps at once.

How many steps should a Korean skincare routine have?

As few as three: cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen. The ten-step routine was a Western marketing frame rather than a Korean rule — most Koreans use a handful of products daily and add extras occasionally.

How often should you exfoliate in a Korean routine?

Once or twice a week for most people, up to three times for oily and resilient skin. Frequency matters far more than strength — a mild acid used consistently beats a strong one that forces you to stop.
